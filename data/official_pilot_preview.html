Emerging Market Credit Shows Resilience amid Energy Shock

原題: Emerging Market Credit Shows Resilience amid Energy Shock

英語原文抜粋（翻訳未取得）

When the Iran war triggered a surge in oil prices this year, many investors may have expected credit markets in the developing world to slide into a selloff. Instead, emerging market sovereign and corporate bonds weathered the shock with a newfound stability and solid performance for investors. The development is a key sign that emerging market credit is maturing into a resilient asset class, according to Anupam Damani, co-head and senior portfolio manager of emerging market debt at Goldman Sachs Asset Management. At the same time, investors are rethinking their approach to the market, which has trillions of dollars in outstanding debt. “Emerging market credit is now unrecognizable, and I mean that in a good way,” Damani says. “These bonds used to come with a crisis label, but this market is no longer monolithic. In my view, it has become a more diverse and resilient asset class, though risks remain.” To be sure, emerging market bonds run the gamut in terms of risk, and investors must be selective about what they trade in a marketplace with myriad issuers, she adds.

The Rise of Healthcare Consumerism | Morgan Stanley

原題: The Rise of Healthcare Consumerism | Morgan Stanley

英語原文抜粋（翻訳未取得）

U.S. consumers are increasingly taking the lead in how they access and pay for healthcare, creating potential opportunities across biopharma and healthcare services. In the complex and costly U.S. healthcare system, patients are no longer just heeding drug ads to “Ask Your Doctor”—they’re going straight to the source. Policy changes and technological advances have given consumers more ways to directly choose the healthcare products and services that best suit their needs—including care, testing and medicines—as well as how they pay for them. The healthcare industry is adapting to this trend, potentially creating new opportunities for investors. In biopharma alone, the total direct-to-consumer cash-pay market could reach $26 billion at its peak. Eyeing that opportunity, many large-cap biopharma companies now offer direct-to-consumer platforms. Healthcare consumerism took root with the creation of Health Savings Accounts (HSAs) in 2003, followed by the growth of high-deductible health plans (HDHPs). These changes gave consumers more control over healthcare spending—but also shifted more of the costs and decisions to them.

Higher yields have changed the fixed income opportunity. More of a portfolio's potential return can come from income, even as persistent inflation tests bonds' traditional diversification role and diverging economic cycles create different investment conditions across markets. The challenge is no longer simply finding yield, but understanding which risks investors are being paid to take. Income may be abundant, but it is not interchangeable; similar yields can carry very different risks. With policy rates and longer-term yields moving higher, investors are weighing how much risk higher rates pose to different parts of the economy. Growth remains resilient overall, but rate sensitivity varies significantly across sectors and households, while some inflation pressures are less responsive to rates. Today’s starting yields may allow investors to generate attractive income without reaching as far for it. Investors are questioning what is keeping long-term yields elevated and what that means for bonds in portfolios. Inflation is only part of the answer: stronger nominal growth, greater competition for capital and diminished diversification benefits are also contributing to higher yields. That combination strengthens fixed income’s role as a source of income, even as persistent inflation makes its diversification benefits more conditional. Higher European bond yields have improved the income opportunity, but tighter spreads leave less room for broad spread compression to drive returns. That shifts the focus toward relative value across corporate issuers and capital structures, while diverging fiscal and political paths create different considerations for sovereign investors.
